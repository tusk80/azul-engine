Machine learning benchmark · two brains, one search

The neural network wins.

The engine can judge positions two ways: with classic rules written and tuned by hand, or with a neural network that learned from 1.5 million positions the engine played against itself, then from its own games. We had them play each other 3,000 times, with the same thinking time.

Speed is strength

A network judges better than hand-written rules but costs more per position, so in the same time it looks at fewer of them. Its first version took 2,161 ns per position against the classic engine's 309. Running its first layer the way chess engines do, updating only the inputs a move changes, in exact integer arithmetic, brought that to 704 ns: the same network gained almost 50 Elo. Elo over the classic engine at 40 ms per move; lines show the 95% range.

It taught itself

The first network learned from games the classic engine played. Then the network played 10,000 games against itself, a new network learned from them, and it had to beat its parent to take over. One generation of self-play was worth +82 ± 13 Elo over its parent. Then it levelled off: the next three generations could not beat it. A second run broke the plateau by fine-tuning each network from its parent with a smaller step and 20,000 games per generation. Generation 7 beat generation 1 by +83 ± 13 over 3,000 games. A third run added three more small steps: generation 10 beat generation 7 by +38 ± 12 over 3,000 games and is the board's brain. Against the classic engine the gain has levelled off around +165; the newer networks fix mistakes the classic engine does not punish. Elo over the classic engine at 40 ms per move.

Against an independent AlphaZero

Beating its own classic engine says little about strength against anyone else. So the engine played cestpasphoto/alpha-zero-general, an open-source AlphaZero (Monte Carlo tree search guided by a deep network) with a strong pretrained Azul model, in that project's own game simulator. Every deal was played twice with the seats swapped. At its default 100 simulations per move it took 12% of the points; at 800, thinking longer than this engine, 18.5%. Elo for this engine, 200 games each; lines show the 95% range.

Fast enough to use what it knows

Before the speed-up the bigger network was too slow to use its knowledge: +6 at equal time. Now each network plays about as well at equal time as at equal positions. Head to head, 128 × 32 and 64 × 16 tie (−10 ± 12 over 3,000 games), so the board keeps the smaller, lighter one.

What it sees matters most

Given only the board, the network merely tied. Showing it the board as it would look if the round were scored now was worth 150 Elo.

What it learns from

Learning half from the search's opinion and half from who actually won beat either alone.

How it was built

  1. Play. The classic engine played 30,000 games against itself; every position became a row of 170 numbers with what happened next.
  2. Learn. A network with two small hidden layers learned to predict the final score margin. It was trained twice: from scratch in plain Go (no libraries), and with PyTorch on a GPU.
  3. Plug in. The network replaces the hand-written judgement inside the same search, so the only difference between the two engines is the brain.
  4. Teach itself. The network played itself; a new network learned from those games and took over only after beating its parent.
  5. Speed up. Consecutive positions in a search differ by a move or two, so the network's first layer is updated rather than recomputed, in integers so it never drifts. Three times faster, exactly the same answers.
  6. Prove it. Thousands of games at equal positions and at equal time, every deal played from both seats.

Which should you use?

Neural network

The stronger player, and the default. Use it when you want the best move.

Classic

Every term is a readable rule you can look up. Use it to see how a hand-built judgement thinks, or to compare the two on a position.
